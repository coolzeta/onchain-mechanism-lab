## From assets to NAV per unit

Use the fictional Harbor Note asset and token quantities, separately assuming the fund-unit structure from lesson one and a separate fee scenario: the tokenized fund has 1,000 units outstanding. Its short-term government securities and cash are worth $100,000 in total, and it owes $2,000 in accrued expenses, with no other liabilities. At this simplified point in time:

```text
$100,000 assets − $2,000 liabilities = $98,000 net assets
NAV per unit = $98,000 ÷ 1,000 = $98
```

The lab below displays **total assets divided by tokens** and does not deduct liabilities. With these assets and units, it shows $100, not the $98 NAV above. It does not accrue fees, estimate returns, or simulate an actual payment.

## Record one period of changes

Continuing the example, assume $4,000 of interest actually arrives and was not already included in the asset value above. The fund pays a separate $1,000 fee that was not included in the prior accrued expense. With no other changes, assets become $103,000; the original $2,000 liability remains, leaving $101,000 in net assets, or $101 per unit. This is a hypothetical period amount, not an annualized return or forecast.

If a fee was already accrued as a liability, paying it reduces assets and liabilities together; do not subtract it from NAV a second time. Similarly, when interest already included in valuation is received as cash, it generally should not be counted as new income again. A distribution can reduce fund assets and NAV per unit, while reinvestment can retain value in the fund. Terms, timing, and taxes can affect either path.

## Reference value, trading price and redemption

Even a calculated $101 NAV does not mean a buyer will pay that price on a secondary market. Bids, trading costs and transfer restrictions affect execution; redemption pricing dates, fees, eligibility and settlement time depend on the actual rules. The lab's assumed asset value is not an independently verified NAV.
